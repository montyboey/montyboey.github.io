1. The 3 C's Learning Cycle

Most students struggle not from a lack of intellect, but from mistaking familiarity for true retention. Passive habits (such as highlighting pages, re-reading passages, or copying lecture slides word-for-word) give an illusion of competence. Real learning requires working through the continuous three-stage cycle:

Phase Core Objective Actionable Rule
1. Collect Filter high-yield concepts rather than acting like a sponge. Ask: "Why does this matter?" If you cannot pinpoint why an idea is essential, avoid writing it down until you possess sufficient context.
2. Connect Form schemas and link new ideas into an interconnected web. Memory is a web, not a filing cabinet. Draw concept maps, flowcharts, or visual diagrams rather than recording isolated sentences.
3. Challenge Test your understanding against reality through deliberate friction. Complete practice problems without looking at solutions. Explain ideas simply from memory. Real learning should feel mentally demanding.

2. Active Recall & The Testing Effect

Every time your brain searches its memory banks to retrieve an answer without looking at the source, the underlying neural pathways grow stronger. Passive exposure does not build long-term memory; active retrieval does.

How to Implement Active Recall

  • The Closed-Book Blurting Method: Read a section of study material once. Close the source entirely. Take a blank sheet of paper and write down everything you remember. Open the book in a different coloured pen and fill in the omissions.
  • Question-Driven Notes: Instead of writing statements (e.g. "Von Neumann uses a single bus"), phrase your notes as questions (e.g. "What is the key bottleneck of the Von Neumann architecture?"). When reviewing, quiz yourself before revealing the answer.
  • Exam-Writer Perspective: For every concept you study, ask yourself: "If I were writing an exam question to trip students up on this topic, how would I design it?"

4. High-Yield Note-Taking Principles

Notes are meant to serve as an extension of your thinking, not a passive transcription service.

  • Avoid Walls of Text: Never transcribe verbatim sentences. Translate blocks of text into diagrams, flowcharts, or structured tables.
  • Accept Cognitive Load: If you write down everything the lecturer says immediately, the information bypasses your brain entirely. Hold the idea in your working memory, chew on it, synthesise it, and only then record the core takeaway.
  • Iterative Versions: Notes taken in class are version one. Version two adds missing links after completing practice tasks. Version three condenses the topic onto a single reference sheet.
  • Learn Technical Concepts in Words First: When approaching mathematical or engineering equations, explain the underlying mechanism in plain words before memorising formulas or plugging in numbers.
