Module 1: The Communication System Architecture

The goal of any communication system is to transport a message from a source to a destination efficiently and reliably over a physical medium.

Core Components

  • Transducer (Input): Converts physical messages (voice, temperature, light) into an electrical signal.
  • Transmitter: Modulates, amplifies, and couples the signal onto the communication channel.
  • Channel: The physical medium (air, copper wire, fiber optic cable). Real-world channels introduce attenuation, distortion, and noise.
  • Receiver: Demodulates the signal, filters out noise, and reconstructs the original electrical waveform.
  • Transducer (Output): Converts the electrical signal back into a physical message (e.g., a speaker producing sound).

Key Signal Processing Goals

  • Channel Analysis: Understanding required bandwidth and how signals degrade.
  • Compression: Using frequency-domain techniques (like MP3 or JPEG) to store signals efficiently.
  • Multiplexing: Sending multiple distinct signals simultaneously on different frequency bands to share a single channel.

Module 2: Fourier Series & Harmonics

The Fourier Series states that any periodic signal can be represented as a sum of fundamental sinusoidal waves (sine and cosine) at different frequencies and amplitudes.

The Fourier Series Equation

g(t) = a0 + ∑ (an cos(nω0t) + bn sin(nω0t))

Where:

  • a0 is the DC component (average value of the signal).
  • ω0 is the fundamental angular frequency (2π / T0).
  • n is an integer representing the harmonic number.

Understanding Harmonics

Harmonics are the frequency components of a periodic signal that occur at integer multiples of the fundamental frequency.

  • 1st Harmonic (Fundamental): n=1, frequency = f0.
  • 2nd Harmonic: n=2, frequency = 2f0.
  • 3rd Harmonic: n=3, frequency = 3f0.

Module 3: Channels & Additive White Gaussian Noise

In the real world, a transmitted signal s(t) never arrives perfectly. The most common mathematical model for channel degradation is the AWGN Channel.

AWGN (Additive White Gaussian Noise)

r(t) = s(t) + n(t)
  • Additive: The noise is simply added to the transmitted signal.
  • White: The noise power is spread evenly across all frequencies (like white light contains all colors).
  • Gaussian: The amplitude of the noise follows a normal (bell-curve) distribution in the time domain.

Module 4: Correlator Demodulation

The receiver's job is to extract the original message from the noisy received signal r(t). The Correlator Demodulator achieves this by converting the continuous waveform into an N-dimensional vector of sufficient statistics.

The Correlation Process

  1. Multiply: The received signal r(t) is multiplied by a known basis function ψ1(t) (the exact shape of the expected transmitted pulse).
  2. Integrate: The product is integrated over the duration of the bit period Tb.
  3. Sample: The result of the integral is a single numerical value, r, which represents the projection of the received signal onto that basis function.

Because white noise is random and has a zero mean, integrating it over time effectively averages it out, while the true signal energy accumulates.

Module 5: Power Spectral Density (PSD) of Line Codes

Line codes define how digital bits are represented as physical voltage pulses on a wire (e.g., Return-to-Zero, Non-Return-to-Zero, Manchester). The Power Spectral Density tells us how the power of that line code is distributed across the frequency spectrum, dictating the required channel bandwidth.

PSD Mathematical Model

The PSD of a line coding scheme is determined by the shape of the basic pulse v(t) and the statistical properties of the random symbol sequence an.

  • v(t): The unit amplitude pulse shape (duration Tb).
  • V(f): The Fourier transform of the basic pulse.
  • μa = E[an]: The average (mean) amplitude of the symbols.
  • σa2: The variance of the random symbol sequence.

Different line codes shape the PSD differently. For example, Manchester encoding shifts power away from DC (0 Hz), making it suitable for channels that cannot carry DC signals.
